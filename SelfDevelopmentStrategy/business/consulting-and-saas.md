# Consulting and micro-SaaS

Everything here is contingent on a funnel that may not deliver, and that is stated
up front rather than discovered. **Stages are entered on evidence, never on elapsed
time.** A stage whose exit criteria are unmet stays unexited however many months
pass. The calendar does not promote.

## The consulting ladder

| Stage | What it is | Exits when |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Simulated** | Internal work against realistic public workflows | A workflow is documented end to end from public information with named steps, estimated frequency and estimated time cost; the discovery script exists and has been rehearsed; and **the document stands on its own in front of someone who has never met you** as proof you can document a workflow |
| **2 — Free or cheap pilot** | Strictly scoped, real external party | A real party's process is **measurably** different afterwards; the before/after baseline was measured, not estimated; the scope was fixed in writing before work started and was not exceeded |
| **3 — Paid fixed-scope** | Money changes hands | The delivered outcome matches a written scope, and you can state and defend a payback period in months |
| **4 — Repeatable offer** | The same shape twice | A fixed-scope, fixed-price description exists that a buyer can accept without a bespoke proposal |
| **5 — Productization candidate** | Repeated pain → possible SaaS | One shape of pain across enough independent buyers to meet the evidence thresholds — **or** the framework returns an explicit non-verdict |

**Stage 1 is load-bearing and must never be cut.** For the first several months it is
the *expected* evidence path, not a contingency.

## Qualification checklist

Applied to a **reply**, not to a feeling. Replies arrive in unhelpful shapes — a
courteous no, an unsubscribe, a gatekeeper's deflection, a "circle back in the
autumn" — and none of them is a booked call. Qualification that gates on how *warm* a
reply sounds will keep mistaking politeness for movement.

1. A named person confirmed a specific process is painful, in their words.
2. That person can describe its frequency **without being prompted**.
3. Someone has quantified, or can quantify, what it currently costs.
4. **There is a budget signal** — a number, an existing tool spend, a stated approval
   path. Not enthusiasm.
5. The systems involved can actually be accessed, and this person can grant it.
6. Scope can be written in one paragraph a buyer would sign.

Fewer than four of six answered means the next action is **another conversation, not
an offer.** Item 4 is the one most often skipped, and it is what the first kill
criterion is written about.

## ROI method

The only pattern used here:

```text
time saved (hours/week)
  × fully loaded hourly cost      (salary × roughly 1.3–1.5 for benefits
                                   and overhead)
  × 52 weeks
  − platform cost − maintenance cost
  = annual net, expressed as a PAYBACK PERIOD IN MONTHS
```

Two failure modes are designed out. Buyers treat **unloaded** cost estimates as
naive, so the multiplier is not optional. And they treat **industry-average ROI
percentages** as vendor copy — such figures are sector means no particular small
business sees itself in — so no percentage is quoted, ever.

What earns belief is a **measured** baseline and a method shown in full, not a large
number. Report a sensitivity too: the payback recomputed at half the assumed hours
saved. A number that survives halving is a different claim from one that does not.

## Offer structure

Fixed scope, fixed price, one outcome, one paragraph. **Exclusions written before the
price.** Hourly billing is defensible only for genuinely undefined discovery work,
capped at 10–20 hours when used at all.

Once the price is fixed, the one thing still free to move is scope — which makes
scoping and pricing a single act, and holding it tight is the difference between a
cheap pilot you can afford to finish and one that quietly becomes free labour.

Reference bands, from vendor-marketing sources and therefore likely to reflect asking
rates rather than realised ones: fixed projects cluster around EUR/USD 5k–25k, with
10k–15k typical for a four-to-six week build. **Orientation, not a rate card** — you
have no realised transaction to calibrate against until stage 3.

## The case study's refusal rule

The strictest rule in this file: **pilot tracking must refuse to produce a case-study
artifact until a measured before/after number exists.** An unverified time-savings
claim is worse than no case study, because it is the one document a buyer will
actually check.

## The agency side-quest

Subcontracting through automation and AI agencies is real and common, and it opens at
stage 2/3 — **after a pilot case study exists** — rather than as a zero-to-one
shortcut.

The reason is structural. Every account of how you get in says entry runs through
warm channels, and an agency wants proof of work before putting its own client
relationships behind you. The route swaps one cold audience for a harder one, and
what it demands at the door is the artifact you hoped to skip. **A channel that
consumes proof of work is no alternative to building it.**

Target deliberately small: contact 3–5 niche agencies and write down what each one
asks for.

## Opportunity scorecard — nine dimensions

Scored 0–5. **Every score cites its evidence**, never intuition alone.

| Dimension | Axis | What the score answers |
|---|---|---|
| Pain severity | value | What does the current process cost in money, errors or missed opportunity? 0 is a mild annoyance; 5 is a named person's job measurably worse every week |
| Frequency | value | How often? The multiplier in every ROI calculation, and the commonest place a plausible automation turns out worthless |
| Labour cost | value | Hours per occurrence × fully loaded rate |
| Process predictability | feasibility | How rule-based and standardised? A process that differs every time is not automatable at this scale |
| Data availability | feasibility | Is the input machine-readable and reachable, or does it live in someone's inbox and head? |
| Integration feasibility | feasibility | Do the systems have APIs, and can the buyer actually grant access? **Permission friction kills more pilots than technical difficulty** |
| Buyer access | reachability | Can you reach the person who decides? |
| Willingness to pay | reachability | Is there an actual conversation or an observable signal behind it? **Never an assumption** |
| Market repetition | value | How many other businesses have this same shape of problem? The bridge from consulting to product |

**How to score:**

1. Read the workflow document first. A dimension scored before the workflow exists is
   scored against a memory of a conversation.
2. Write the evidence beside the number — a quotation, a document reference, an
   observed signal. One line per dimension.
3. With no evidence, still score it, and mark it **`assumed`**. An assumed score is
   legitimate; an assumed score dressed as an evidenced one is not.
4. **Never average the nine.** The profile's *shape* carries the decision, and a mean
   hides a 0 on integration feasibility behind four 4s.
5. Re-score rather than adjust, and keep both scorings.

Buyer access and willingness to pay are the two most likely to be scored wishfully,
and willingness to pay is what the first kill criterion is about: a problem called
annoying and then not paid for is the commonest way a promising score empties out.

**The reproducibility test.** Score one opportunity twice, a month apart, from the
recorded evidence alone with the originals covered. The two profiles should land in
the same place, and every gap should trace to evidence that arrived in between —
never to a change of mood. Movements with new evidence behind them are the
opportunity changing; movements without are **the instrument failing**, and they are
what tells you which dimension's wording needs rewriting.

Three real dimensions are deliberately **not** scored, noted so their absence reads
as a decision: *exception-handling burden* (folding it in would change what
predictability means), *change and maintenance risk* (a durability question, and
these nine are a viability set), and *compliance sensitivity* (some processes are
automation-unfriendly regardless of value). Use them as a written sanity note beside
the profile. If one of them would have changed the decision, **say so** — that is
exactly the signal worth having when the scorecard is next revised.

## Micro-SaaS: evidence before building

The chain is: consulting → repeated pain → evidence → prototype → paid validation →
productization. **Micro-SaaS is not a compulsory outcome, and no idea gets forced.**

### Evidence thresholds

| Threshold | N | Why that number |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interviews | 8 | Below 8 independent conversations, the same pain appearing twice is as likely to be coincidence as signal |
| Problem observed repeatedly | 3 | The same shape of pain in 3 **independent** businesses. Not 3 mentions by one buyer, and not 3 businesses introduced by the same source |
| Users willing to pilot | 3 | Two can be politeness from a warm-ish contact. Three independent yeses is the smallest number where one no does not collapse the evidence |
| Payment or strong purchase commitment | 1 | Money is the only evidence here that politeness cannot produce. One is enough because it is **categorical, not statistical** |
| Clear acquisition channel | — | A named channel with a **measured** cost per conversation, taken from your own funnel. A channel that has not been measured is a hope |
| Estimated ROI | — | Computed from a measured baseline by the fully-loaded method, as a payback period. An industry-average percentage does not satisfy this |
| Manual workflow proven first | 1 | At least one concierge delivery, by hand. Fail to produce the outcome by hand for a single customer and all automation buys you is an industrialised way of failing |

The last two carry no count because a count is the wrong instrument: both are
satisfied by a measurement existing at all, and a number would invite repetition
rather than one measurement made properly.

Raising a threshold on evidence is fine. **Lowering one requires writing down why,
and the impulse to lower one is itself information about the evidence you actually
have.**

### Kill criteria

Each with its detection rule attached, because a kill criterion you cannot observe is
a worry rather than a criterion.

| Criterion | How it is detected |
|---|---|
| People say it is annoying but will not pay | Interview notes record enthusiasm and no budget signal across ≥3 conversations |
| The workflow differs completely between companies | The documented workflows share fewer than half their steps, so any product is really N bespoke builds |
| Acquisition cost is obviously too high | Your own measured funnel implies a cost per qualified conversation exceeding a plausible first-year contract value |
| Integration permissions make deployment impractical | Two or more prospects cannot or will not grant the access the automation requires |
| An existing product already solves 90% of the pain cheaply | Competition analysis finds a product at a price the buyer would accept, covering the workflow's main path |

### The prototype gate

Three conditions, all required at once:

1. The opportunity sits above your shortlist threshold on **all nine** dimensions,
   with evidence cited per dimension.
2. No kill criterion fires.
3. Buyer access and willingness to pay rest on an **actual conversation or an
   observable signal**, not an assumption.

**The gate has an explicit no branch, and no is the likely answer for a long time.**
A gate with no way to say no is a formality with a checkbox on it.

### The non-verdict is a passing deliverable

"Insufficient evidence, deferred to month 7" **passes**, provided it names which
threshold is short and the month that evidence could realistically arrive.
"More research needed" is not a non-verdict; it is the absence of one.

That permission is not softness. **A framework obliged to produce a score will
produce one, leaning the way its author already wanted** — which, after months of
building toward a product, is forward. Being allowed to say *not enough to decide
yet* also removes the pressure to manufacture evidence when the funnel underdelivers,
and underdelivery is the expected case.

A scorecard that has only ever produced yeses is a rationalisation engine.

## Evidence discipline

Everything here carries `real` or `simulated`, and the two are never added together.
An ROI calculation from a stage-1 baseline is `simulated` and passes; the same
calculation presented as a client result is a plan failure.

**Paid pilots and revenue are never simulated.** Both are expected to read zero for a
long time, and both are recorded anyway — a scoreboard that drops its hardest measure
has stopped measuring.

Real client names, real prices and real contact details live in gitignored
`*.local.md` files. Tracked copies carry placeholder identities, so this repository
can be made public at any moment with no scrub pass.
